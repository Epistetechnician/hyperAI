# A practical wedge for physical AI

*A working business thesis for discussion · October 2026*

I don't think the first crypto × robotics opportunity is decentralizing
frontier model training. The practical opening, to me, is making physical data
useful: can a robot team see what its records cover, what is missing, and
whether they answer a specific decision?

My hypothesis is that teams lose time cleaning and joining robot logs, demos,
and tests. Hours aren't utility: the valuable unit is evidence tied to one
robot, policy, task, and set of conditions. That's a question to validate,
not a market fact.

## Where I'd start

I'd start with an OEM or integrator running repeated manipulation tasks. For
one release or failure question, we take a customer-controlled historical
export and return a versioned packet linking robot and policy, task, outcomes,
exposure, and conditions. It marks incomplete lineage, missing denominators,
failed runs, interventions, and unknowns.

The first offer is an 8–10 week, fixed-scope data-readiness and evidence
review. USD 20,000–35,000 is a price hypothesis, not a market rate. The review
shows where records are out of sync or conditions are missing; it does not
claim model improvement, training readiness, or robot safety. A specific gap
can become a buyer-approved evidence request. We don't need to own or pool the
underlying data.

Products and internal tools already touch robot failures, fleet monitoring,
and data quality. This only works if it solves a concrete gap they leave and a
buyer will pay for it.

## How it could grow

I'd start service-assisted, standardize the checks that repeat, and sell the
workflow across releases and sites. Integrators or robot service operators
could become delivery partners if the workflow repeats and data rights are
clear. Any advantage comes from reusable methods, not default access to
customer records.

Crypto doesn't solve the first problem. Later, it may help multiple parties
coordinate attribution, rights, or settlement. It won't prove sensor truth or
robot safety, or make frontier training easy to distribute. I'd use ordinary
contracts and payments until a real coordination need appears.

## What would change my mind

I want to speak with 15–20 teams and review five data schemas. I'd move toward
a pilot only if three independent organizations describe the same costly
problem, have a lawful read-only path to useful records, and at least two
consider paying. Measure preparation and delivery time, coverage gaps, cost,
and repeat business. If the data don't answer the question, existing tools
already solve it, or buyers won't pay, we should narrow or stop.

With partners, I want to pressure-test who owns the budget, what evidence is
missing, and whether an integrator can make this workflow repeatable.

This is a business hypothesis, not a launched product or validated plan. The
first pilot would not control robots, train a model, buy data, or publish
customer results.
